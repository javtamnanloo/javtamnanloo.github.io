Pulling a molecular ring open

Quantum-chemistry simulations of a strained carbon ring torn open by force, and of what it does when the force lets go.

Most chemical reactions are started with heat or light. Mechanochemistry uses force instead. When a long polymer chain is stretched hard, for example by the collapsing bubbles of ultrasound in a polymer solution, the pull concentrates on the bonds along its backbone, and a small, strained ring built into that backbone can be torn open.

The ring studied here is gem-difluorocyclopropane (gDFC): a triangle of three carbon atoms with two fluorine atoms on one corner. In 2010, Lenhardt and coworkers built these rings into polybutadiene and showed that pulling on the polymer opens them into a 1,3-diradical, a molecule with two unpaired electrons. Normally this structure is the transition state of the ring’s isomerization and lasts for a single molecular vibration, but the pull holds it open: the transition state is trapped by tension [1]. In 2015, Wang and coworkers asked what the ring does once the force is released [2]. The simulations below follow both steps atom by atom, with the electrons treated by quantum mechanics.

Why the fluorines matter

A three-membered ring is already strained: its bond angles are squeezed to 60°, far from the roughly 109° that carbon prefers. Two fluorine atoms on one corner add more strain and weaken the carbon–carbon bond on the opposite side of the triangle [1]. That bond, the one that breaks in the movies, gives way much more easily than a bond in plain cyclopropane, so a moderate pull opens the ring without tearing the rest of the molecule apart.

How the movies are made

Breaking a bond leaves two electrons that no longer pair up, and standard density functional theory describes that poorly. The simulations therefore use a multireference method: CASSCF with second-order perturbation corrections (CASPT2), with two electrons in the two orbitals of the breaking bond and the 6-31G* basis set, in OpenMolcas. In ab initio molecular dynamics, the atoms move by Newton’s laws while the forces on them come from this quantum calculation, repeated every 0.25 to 0.5 femtoseconds. Each run starts from positions and velocities sampled from the molecule’s quantum vibrational (Wigner) distribution at 280 K. The polymer chain on either side of the ring is replaced by a constant pulling force on the two hydrogens where the chain would attach, drawn in orange in the movies. For the ring-closing runs, the force is switched off.

In each movie:

  • Left chart: the length of the breaking bond. An intact carbon–carbon bond is about 1.5 Å long.
  • Right chart: how that bond’s two electrons are shared between its bonding and antibonding orbitals. An intact bond keeps both in the bonding orbital (about 2 and 0). As the bond breaks, the electrons split up, one in each orbital (about 1 and 1): the diradical.
  • The stick for the breaking bond is drawn from those same numbers, so it thins and disappears as the bond breaks and grows back as it forms. A faint dotted line keeps showing the distance once the bond is gone.
  • Time: one second of video is 30 femtoseconds (3 × 10⁻¹⁴ s) of the simulation.
  • Motion: the atom positions are smoothed over a few femtoseconds to calm the fastest hydrogen vibrations, so the slower twisting is easier to follow. The charts show the unsmoothed data.

Pulling the ring open

Pulling the two orange hydrogens apart stretches the bond opposite the fluorine corner until it breaks, here after about 190 femtoseconds. As it gives way, the two ends of the bond, each a carbon carrying two hydrogens, twist in opposite directions, one clockwise and one counterclockwise (a disrotatory opening). With the force on, the open diradical stays open: the pull has turned a fleeting transition state into a stable structure.

Letting go

In the experiment the force lasts only as long as a bubble collapse. Once it is gone, the diradical is a transition state again, and the ring snaps shut, usually within about 100 femtoseconds.

By far the more likely outcome is that the two ends twist in opposite directions again (disrotatory), and the ring closes back into the form it started from.

Much less often, both ends twist the same way (conrotatory), and the ring closes into a slightly different form, with one orange hydrogen flipped over. In this run the diradical lingers for about 200 femtoseconds before it finds that exit.

Why it matters

Transition states decide how fast a reaction runs and which products it makes, but they normally last about 10⁻¹³ s, far too short to study directly. Tension trapping holds one in place, and these simulations show, femtosecond by femtosecond, how a held transition state falls apart once it is let go.
