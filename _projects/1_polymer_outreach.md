Akron, the Rubber City, sits at the heart of Ohio's Polymer Valley, and the University of Akron's School of Polymer Science and Polymer Engineering is ranked first in the United States for polymer science by [EduRank](https://edurank.org/chemistry/polymers/us). As part of the School's Third Frontier outreach group, I help introduce students to polymers, using the same kind of simulations I run in my research alongside hands-on experiments.

## What is a polymer?

A polymer is a very long molecule made by linking many small units, called monomers, into a chain, like beads on a string. Plastics, rubber, and nylon are polymers, and so are the DNA and proteins in our bodies. Because the chains are so long, they coil up and tangle with each other like a bowl of cooked spaghetti, and this tangling gives polymers much of their strength and stretch.

## Longer chains hold together longer

Each strip is a computer simulation of a small piece of polymer melt, a soft material with no crosslinks between its chains, much like Silly Putty. All four are pulled at the same speed; the only difference is the length of the chains, from 20 to 200 units. Short chains barely tangle, so they slide past each other and the sample tears apart early. Longer chains are more entangled: they have to slide and straighten out before the sample can come apart, so it draws out into long fibers first. This is one reason longer chains make tougher plastics.

## Pull slowly and it stretches; pull fast and it snaps

<figure>
  <video src="{{ '/assets/video/pulling_speed.mp4' | relative_url }}" poster="{{ '/assets/img/outreach/pulling_speed.jpg' | relative_url }}" autoplay muted loop playsinline aria-label="Simulations of a 200-unit polymer melt pulled slowly, five times faster, and ten times faster" style="width: 100%; border-radius: 6px; background: #000;"></video>
  <figcaption>The same 200-unit chains pulled at three speeds.</figcaption>
</figure>

Pulled slowly, the chains have time to slide past each other and line up along the pulling direction, so the sample keeps stretching. Pulled five or ten times faster, they have no time to adapt: the sample narrows, snaps, and some chains break. You can feel the same thing with Silly Putty: pull it slowly and it stretches, yank it and it breaks.

## Polymer Explorers at the library

In June 2026, with the School's outreach team, I helped run Polymer Explorers, a hands-on workshop for high-school students at the Akron-Summit County Public Library's Main Library. Students moved through four stations, including Stretch or Snap?, which paired the simulations above with Silly Putty, and my Slimy Worms station, where sodium alginate squeezed into a calcium chloride bath instantly forms rubbery hydrogel worms as calcium ions crosslink the chains.
